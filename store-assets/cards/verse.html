The speaker asks whether he should compare his beloved to a summer's day, immediately deciding the beloved is superior because a summer day is flawed.

Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?

The speaker poses a question to himself, considering whether to use a standard poetic comparison of a person to the height of summer, using the phrase "summer's day" to represent natural perfection.

Thou art more lovely and more temperate:

The speaker answers his own question, claiming the beloved surpasses summer because she is both physically more beautiful ("more lovely") and emotionally or physically more balanced and mild ("more temperate").

Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,

The speaker begins to prove summer's flaws, noting that harsh weather damages the fragile, newly formed flowers ("darling buds") that belong to the start of the summer season.

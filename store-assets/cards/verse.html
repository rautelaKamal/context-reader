VerseShakespeare, Sonnet 18

Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
Thou art more lovely and more temperate:
Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,

Plain meaningWhat’s the point?Line by lineHow it works

The speaker considers comparing his beloved to a summer day, immediately decides she is superior because summer has flaws, and uses the first of those flaws—violent winds ruining new flowers—to prove it.

Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?

The speaker opens by asking a rhetorical question, proposing to measure the person he addresses against the traditional gold standard of beauty: a day in summer.

Thou art more lovely and more temperate:

He answers his own question instantly with the claim that the person addressed beats summer on two counts: she is more attractive ("more lovely") and more balanced or mild ("more temperate").

Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,

He begins to list summer's faults to justify his claim, pointing out the evidence in "Rough winds" that violently damage the fragile new blossoms ("darling buds") that appear at the start of the season in May.
